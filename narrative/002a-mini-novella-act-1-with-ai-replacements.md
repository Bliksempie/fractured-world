## Ash and Rain

The world stank of old rain and ash.

Elias adjusted the fraying strap on his shoulder, the battered pack digging into skin that hadn't known comfort in years. Beside him, his son trudged silently; a smaller figure, hollow-eyed but unbroken. His name was Brandon. Sometimes it slipped his mind in the fatigue of survival, but tonight, in the mist, he remembered.

Names mattered less now than breathing, bleeding, surviving.

The Necromyce rose quiet from the soil, a fungus learning the shape of a world. Hospitals filled, radios choked, and then the lines went dead. The old calendar ended in a whisper no one could trace—only the aftermath remained.

Ahead, the ruins of Old Haven loomed: twisted steel and hollowed concrete, buildings slumped like blind giants in the gloom. Fungal rot strangled the land itself.

Blightspores came first—fast, mindless, loud. A low Hum gathered them into packs, each region humming its own dialect. Anything that failed to echo was unmade.

Gravebloom tendrils and Blightspore colonies clung to every surface like desperate hands.

Then came the Gravebloomers, who kept their minds and sharpened them. They built doctrine from ruin and patience from hunger, and with each child born into spores, what was human slipped another finger from the ledge.

Elias scanned the road. No Hum. No shuffling silhouettes.

But quiet, in this world, was never trustable.

“Dad,” Brandon said, voice barely above the wind. His boots squelched with every step, soaked through, him spirit yet stubbornly intact.

“Is the safe place… real?”

The uninfected endure in pockets and myths. No cure. No saviors. Only the ledger of choices and the miles left to spend them.

Elias hesitated. Rumors floated like smoke about a colony tucked deep in the Iron Hills: a sanctuary beyond the reach of the Gravebloomers, beyond the mindless Blightspores. But rumors were smoke; thick enough to choke on, never solid enough to hold.

“It's real enough to walk toward,” Elias answered.

Brandon nodded. It was enough.

They pressed on, steps swallowed by the broken road.

## Hollowpoint

By what passed for dusk, when the sun was only a bruise behind the clouds, they reached Hollowpoint. The town had been a ghost long before the bloom had claimed the world.

The mill closed. The kids left. The church bake sales couldn’t sweeten an empty street. By the time the spores came, Hollowpoint had only shutters and silence to lose.

A burned-out pharmacy huddled at the corner of Main and Fifth, its windows shattered, its doors yawning wide like broken jaws. Elias signaled for Brandon to stay low, a silent command he obeyed without question.

They had practiced this dance: Elias points, Brandon nods. Hunger makes choreographers of the desperate.

Inside, the shelves were sparsely filled. Desperation had long since picked the carcass clean.

Yet, in the back, half-buried under rotted beams, the corner of a rusted crate stuck out — a relic of a more stubborn time. Elias strained to lift the beams while Brandon pulled the crate free. Elias pried it open with a grim smile. Inside was a cracked canteen, three unopened protein bars, and, miraculously, a spool of fishing line.

A laughable haul in the old world. A feast in this one.

“We'll eat tonight,” he said, tossing Brandon one of the three unopened protein bars.

## Shriekers in the Dark

The alleys of Hollowpoint twisted like broken veins, shadows snapping at every corner. The Hum fractured into shrieking strands: sharper, hunting tones.

Shriekers.

Thin, wiry beings that ran on all fours, faces locked in twisted agony. Too human to dismiss, too monstrous to forgive.

Elias ducked into the ruins of a bar, Brandon stumbling behind him. He pressed his boy against the wall with a steady hand.

“Breathe through your nose. Stay silent.”

Outside the shattered window, the Shriekers prowled, their ragged keens cutting the night apart. One lifted its face, sniffing. It caught their scent.

A screech. A blur of broken humanity hurtling toward them.

Elias drew his battered knife. “Stay behind me,” he whispered.

And the fight began.

## Blood and Broken Glass

The first Shrieker crashed through the window. Elias sidestepped it, helping it on its way into the sharp edge of a splintered table.

Glass lifted like a silver storm, the body turning weightless for a breath—fingers splayed, mouth open on a sound too big for its throat.

The second circled wide, almost calculating, its claws dragging along broken glass. Elias struck first — a quick slash across its upper arm, a brutal kick to the knee. The creature went down with an agonizing, shrill screech. Elias told Brandon to look away — more ritual than truth; there is no shielding left in this world. Brandon obeyed nevertheless as Elias crushed the Shrieker’s skull with a heavy boot.

The end was a wet wooden crack, a hiss that ran out too soon. The glass around it trembled, then remembered to be still.

## The Broken Road

The overpass was a graveyard of concrete and twisted rebar. No way forward — at least not for the faint-hearted.

Elias found a maintenance catwalk clinging precariously to the edge. Rusted. Dangerous. A deathtrap. But it was the only way. He tested the first steps. Metal groaned. Held. He waved Brandon forward.

Halfway across, a bolt let go with a crack like a knuckle. The span dipped and the handrail shivered loose from its memory of holding. Brandon pitched; Elias caught a fistful of jacket and the world paused on one leg. Rust rained. The metal decided to keep them—for now. They moved again, slower, counting steps like sins.

Finally, after crossing the void — the stars blinking coldly above them, the catwalk a monster in their minds — they set up camp near a stream. Hope was a fragile thing. But they chased it anyway.

Water. Clear, cold, pure. They fell to their knees, drinking like the parched souls they were. They drank in slow, trembling sips until their hands steadied.

Elias built a fire from stubborn will and flint. He caught a fish — thin, but alive — and cooked it carefully, turned it like treasure with the remainder of the three unopened protein bars. They ate in silence, the fire crackling softly beside them.

When Brandon drifted to sleep, Elias watched him for a long time, brushing a lock of hair from his brow.

He whispered into the night:

“Brandon.”

The name cracked the silence, a fragile bridge to a world that had died.

“And me… I’m Elias.”

He leaned against the earth, the fire’s warmth brushing his battered boots.

Sleep came at last, soft as a forgotten lullaby.
